The 1689 Baptist Confession

Of Faith, 1677 and 1689, in plain English

The Second London Baptist Confession of Faith was first published in London in 1677 by the Particular Baptists, the Calvinistic Baptists of England, who were then a persecuted minority. It was printed without names, because its authors could be fined or imprisoned for their worship. In 1689, after the Act of Toleration, messengers from more than a hundred churches met in London and endorsed it, and that is why it is called the 1689. Among those who signed it were William Kiffin, Hanserd Knollys and Benjamin Keach. It was later adopted in America as the Philadelphia Confession, and C. H. Spurgeon republished it for his congregation in 1855. This commentary follows it.

The Baptists did not set out to write a new creed. They took the Westminster Confession of 1647 and the Savoy Declaration of 1658, the Congregationalists’ revision of Westminster, and kept their words wherever they could, to show how much they shared with other Reformed Christians. Each paragraph is given here in plain, modern English, with a short note on what it means. The confession’s own words, with the Bible texts it gave as proof, are folded away under each paragraph. Like every confession, it claims only to say what Scripture says, and it is to be tested by Scripture (1.10).

How it differs from Westminster. Most of the 1689 is word for word the same as the Westminster Confession, but the changes are deliberate. It reshapes the chapter on God’s covenant (7), seeing the covenant of grace promised in the Old Testament and established in the new covenant. It adds a chapter of its own on the gospel (20). It drops Westminster’s grant of power to the civil magistrate over the church (21 and 24). It speaks of baptism and the Lord’s Supper as ordinances rather than sacraments and seals (28), and holds that baptism is for those who profess faith, by immersion (29). It sets out the church as congregations of visible saints, each governed under Christ by its own elders and deacons, with gatherings of churches that advise but do not rule (26), in place of Westminster’s chapters on church discipline and on synods and councils. These places are explained in the chapter introductions, with links to the matching places in Westminster.

On a few points this commentary differs from the 1689 itself, chiefly on the Sabbath (22.7–8) and on naming the Pope as the Antichrist (26.4). Those places are marked This commentary’s view, and the confession’s own text is left as it is.
