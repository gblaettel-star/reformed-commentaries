The Heidelberg Catechism

1563, in plain English

The Heidelberg Catechism was written in 1563 at the request of Frederick III, Elector of the Palatinate in Germany, who wanted one clear and warm statement of the faith for the churches, schools and homes of his lands. It is chiefly the work of two young theologians, Zacharias Ursinus and Caspar Olevianus, with the help of the Heidelberg faculty and ministers. It became one of the most loved confessions of the Reformation, and is still taught in Reformed churches around the world.

It begins with the question of our only comfort in life and in death, and then follows the shape of Paul’s letter to the Romans in three parts: our misery (how great our sin is), our deliverance (how Christ sets us free, taught through the Apostles’ Creed and the sacraments), and our thankfulness (how to live for God, taught through the Ten Commandments and the Lord’s Prayer). Its 129 questions are divided into 52 Lord’s Days, so that the whole could be taught, one portion each Sunday, over a year.

It is still a fine way to use it: one Lord’s Day a week, read slowly, perhaps with family or friends around a table. Each answer is given here in plain, modern English, with a short note on what it means. The catechism’s own words, in the English of Philip Schaff’s The Creeds of Christendom (1877), with some of the Bible texts that support each answer, are folded away under each question.

This commentary is Baptist, following the 1689 London Baptist Confession, and it loves this catechism. Where they differ, above all on the baptism of infants, the place is marked This commentary’s view, and the catechism’s own text is left as it is.
