Calvin’s Institutes in Brief

1559, all 80 chapters

John Calvin first published the Institutes of the Christian Religion in 1536, a small book for French believers under persecution, dedicated to the king of France. For the rest of his life he kept enlarging it, and the final Latin edition of 1559 became the most influential summary of the Reformed faith ever written. Calvin meant it as a guide to reading Scripture well: a map of what the whole Bible teaches, so that readers would know what to look for in it.

Here each of its 80 chapters is condensed: what Calvin teaches, following his argument in order; two or three of his own sentences, quoted from Henry Beveridge’s translation (1845), with the section they come from; and why it matters today. The four books follow the shape of the Apostles’ Creed: God the Creator (Book 1), God the Redeemer in Christ (Book 2), the Spirit applying Christ’s grace to us (Book 3), and the church, the outward means by which God draws us to Christ and keeps us in him (Book 4).

This commentary stands broadly with Calvin. Where it holds a different view, chiefly on baptism, on the covenants and on the magistrate’s power in matters of religion, the place is marked Where this commentary differs.
