The Second Helvetic Confession

Bullinger, 1566, in plain English

The Second Helvetic Confession was written by Heinrich Bullinger, Zwingli’s successor as chief pastor of Zurich, at first as his own personal confession of faith (1562). When the Elector Frederick III of the Palatinate, who had given the church the Heidelberg Catechism, needed to show that his faith was the faith of the catholic church, Bullinger’s confession was published in 1566 and adopted by the Reformed churches of Switzerland. It was soon received or approved across Reformed Europe, from Scotland to Hungary and Poland. Warm, pastoral and full of the early church fathers, it is the most widely received of the Swiss confessions.

Ten years before, Bullinger had set out the same faith for ordinary believers. His Summa christenlicher Religion (1556) was written in German, not Latin, so that households, schoolmasters and country pastors could read it, and it walks through the whole faith in ten short books, from Holy Scripture to the last things. It is plain, warm and pastoral, the teaching of a pastor who wanted his people to know why they believed what they believed. The Second Helvetic Confession says the same faith again, more fully and more formally, as a confession the churches could sign together. Reading it, you are hearing the mature voice of the pastor who first taught it to the families of Zurich.

Each section is given here in plain, modern English, with a short note on what it means. The Confession’s own words are folded away under each section, in the English of Philip Schaff’s Creeds of Christendom (1877), which draws in part on the translation in the Harmony of Confessions (1586). Bible references in the plain English are given in modern form, and a few misprinted references in the old text are corrected there. Like every confession, it claims only to say what Scripture says, and Bullinger himself asked to be corrected from Scripture.

This commentary follows the 1689 Baptist Confession. On baptism, Mary, the church’s order and discipline, and the magistrate’s power in matters of religion, it holds a different view. Those places are marked Where this commentary differs, and the Confession’s own text is left as it is.
